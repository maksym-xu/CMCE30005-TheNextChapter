# Screening methods for the final report

We used the existing sample of 3,873 eligible listings from 1,726 hosts. The outcome was whether a listing received at least 30 dated reviews in the preceding 365 days. Reviews indicate historical guest activity and do not measure occupancy or profit. The three priority groups remained fixed: two-bedroom and three-bedroom apartments in the City of Melbourne, and three-bedroom houses or townhouses in Yarra Ranges. These groups were selected during earlier exploration. All model comparisons retain the same established listings with usable recorded prices. Removing price as a model input does not validate performance for excluded unpriced listings or new rental candidates.

We reused the exact 50 host allocations from the preceding analysis. Each allocation placed approximately 70% of hosts in training and 30% in testing, keeping all listings from a host together. We reused the saved scores from the full six-input logistic model and the simple baseline. The baseline assigns a training target rate to each area, dwelling-type and bedroom group, with the existing fixed smoothing rule. We fitted one additional version of logistic regression on each training set, retaining area, dwelling type, bedrooms and recorded amenity count while excluding current price and minimum-stay settings. This comparison estimates the joint predictive contribution of the two excluded inputs. It does not isolate their individual effects or show what would happen if an operator changed either setting.

We evaluated all eligible test listings and the three priority groups. Within the priority groups, we considered two allocation rules. One combines the three groups into one candidate pool. The other fixes a separate selection quota inside each group before combining results. The second rule tests whether the score improves selection within groups, without gaining from shifting places between groups. All models kept the same training population. We restricted the candidate pool only after scoring the test listings.

For each allocation rule, we selected 10%, 25% or 50% of candidates, rounding the quota up and using identical quotas for all models. Listings above the score boundary received a place. Listings tied at that boundary shared the remaining places equally without reference to their outcomes. We recorded expected target-reaching selections, the share of selected listings reaching the target, and the share of all target-reaching listings captured. We also recorded AUC and Brier score for all eligible test listings and for the pooled priority listings. These two measures do not depend on shortlist size.

Within an individual property group, the simple baseline assigns the same score to every listing. Its expected selection therefore matches uniform random selection within that group. The reduced logistic model can distinguish listings within that group only by amenity count, because its other three inputs are constant. Its use assumes that amenities are already known and measured consistently when a property is screened.

We calculated model differences within each split and then averaged the 50 differences with equal weight. We also reported the fifth and ninety-fifth percentiles and the number of better, tied and worse splits. These percentiles describe sensitivity to the host allocation and are not confidence intervals. The test sets overlap, and the earlier selection of groups and features remains outside this procedure. The checks therefore do not establish future performance or independently validate the complete selection process. The original pooled five-fold presentation result remains a separate result and is not combined with the repeated-split averages.
